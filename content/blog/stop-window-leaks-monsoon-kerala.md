Every monsoon brings the same calls: water on the floor under a window, damp patches on the wall beside it, or a puddle on the sill after a heavy shower. The good news is that most window leaks come from a handful of causes — and most of them can be fixed. Here's how to find where the water is getting in, what you can fix yourself, and when it's time for a better window.

## First, find where the water gets in

Before fixing anything, watch the window during heavy rain — or run a garden hose over it from outside, starting low and slowly moving up. Water usually comes in by one of four routes:

1. **Through the frame or tracks** — water appears on the inside of the frame or on the sill.
2. **Around the frame** — damp patches on the wall beside or below the window.
3. **From above** — water tracks down from the lintel or the sunshade.
4. **Through the glazing** — water appears where the glass meets the frame.

Knowing the route tells you which of the causes below you're dealing with.

## Cause 1: Blocked drainage slots

Sliding windows, and many casements, have small drainage slots in the bottom of the frame that let water entering the track flow back outside. When dust, leaves or construction debris block them, the track fills up like a gutter and overflows into the room.

**Fix:** clear the slots with a thin wire or a soft brush, then pour a cup of water into the track and check it drains outside. Do this before every monsoon.

## Cause 2: Failed sealant around the frame

The joint between the window frame and the wall is the most common leak path of all. Sealant cracks as it ages in the sun, gaps open as walls settle, and some windows were simply never sealed properly. Water then runs behind the frame and shows up as damp plaster.

**Fix:** on a dry day, remove loose or cracked sealant on the outside and reseal with a good exterior-grade sealant that suits the frame material. If the plaster around the window is cracked, repair it first.

## Cause 3: Sills and sunshades that send water inward

A sill should slope away from the window and have a drip groove underneath, so water falls clear of the wall. A flat or back-sloping sill — common in older homes — lets water pool against the frame. In the same way, a sunshade without a drip edge can send water running back along its underside onto the top of the window.

**Fix:** re-screeding a sill so it falls outward, or cutting a drip groove, is a job for a mason — but it often solves "mystery" leaks.

## Cause 4: Worn seals, brushes and gaskets

On sliding windows, the brush seals between the shutters wear down over the years; on casements, rubber gaskets can harden and shrink. Old timber windows have their own version of this problem: the wood shrinks and swells with the seasons, and gaps open up.

**Fix:** seals and gaskets can often be replaced. Timber shutters that have warped badly are usually past saving.

## Cause 5: The window itself

Some windows were never going to keep out wind-driven rain: frames without proper drainage, poorly made corner joints, or lightweight sliders on a wall that takes the full force of the weather. If you have cleared the drainage, resealed the frame and fixed the sill, and the window still leaks, it's time to replace it.

## A pre-monsoon checklist for May

Kerala's south-west monsoon usually arrives around the start of June, so May is the month to check every window:

- Clear the drainage slots and tracks on all sliding windows.
- Look for cracked or missing sealant around frames on the outside.
- Check that sills slope away from the windows.
- Make sure every shutter closes and locks tightly.
- Clear leaves from sunshades and gutters above the windows.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does rain come in through my sliding window?

Usually because the drainage slots in the bottom track are blocked, so water fills the track and spills inside. Worn brush seals and strong wind-driven rain can also push water past sliding shutters.

### Can silicone stop window leakage?

Sealing the gap between the frame and the wall with the right exterior sealant fixes many leaks. But sealant smeared over drainage slots or shutter joints can make things worse by trapping water — so find the cause first.

### Are uPVC windows leak-proof?

Well-made uPVC windows with good gaskets and drainage, installed with a sealed perimeter and a sloping sill, keep monsoon rain out — as our Kollam splash test shows. Installation quality matters as much as the window itself. See [Saint-Gobain uPVC windows](/products/saint-gobain-upvc-windows).

### When should I replace a leaking window instead of repairing it?

If the frame is rotten, warped or corroded, or the window still leaks after the drainage, sealant and sill have been fixed, replacement is usually the better long-term choice.
